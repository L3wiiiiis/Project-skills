# Reliable sources by field

Use this list to decide what to search for and what to trust. Search the sources by name (for example "OpenStax biology photosynthesis") and fetch the page before teaching from it.

## Trust order (highest first)

1. **The learner's own course materials**: slides, syllabus, assigned readings, textbook chapters they upload.
2. **Primary and official sources**: legislation, court decisions, government agencies, standards bodies, official software documentation.
3. **Open textbooks and university course material**: free, peer-reviewed or instructor-written.
4. **Scholarly encyclopedias and peer-reviewed reviews.**
5. **Reputable general references**: Wikipedia is fine for orientation and for finding its cited sources, but check important claims against the sources it cites.

Avoid as a sole source: content farms, AI-generated sites, forums, unsourced blogs, social media, and sites selling something related to the claim.

## By field

| Field | Good sources |
|---|---|
| Any subject (open textbooks) | OpenStax, LibreTexts, MIT OpenCourseWare, Open Textbook Library, Saylor Academy |
| Maths | OpenStax, Paul's Online Math Notes, Khan Academy, NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions |
| Physics, chemistry, biology | OpenStax, LibreTexts, HyperPhysics, Khan Academy, review articles (search the journal) |
| Medicine and health | WHO, national health agencies (e.g. NIH/MedlinePlus, NHS, healthdirect), clinical guidelines, StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf) |
| Philosophy | Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy |
| History | University course pages, national archives and libraries, scholarly encyclopedias (e.g. Britannica), peer-reviewed journals |
| Law | Official legislation sites and case law databases for the learner's jurisdiction (e.g. AustLII, legislation.gov.uk, Cornell LII, CanLII), court and regulator websites |
| Economics, finance, statistics | Central banks, national statistics offices, IMF, World Bank, OECD, OpenStax Economics |
| Programming and software | Official docs for the exact language and version (e.g. docs.python.org, MDN Web Docs), the library's own documentation |
| Writing, editing, grammar | The learner's style guide; otherwise major style guides (Chicago, APA, AP, national government style manuals) and university writing centres (e.g. Purdue OWL) |
| Languages | University language departments, national language institutes, reputable dictionaries |
| Business and management | Open textbooks (OpenStax Business), government small-business agencies, peer-reviewed research |

## Books

- You usually cannot read a book's contents. Teach from what you can access, and recommend books for further reading.
- Before recommending a book, search to confirm the title, author and that it is what you think it is. Prefer standard course textbooks and well-reviewed introductions.
- Free and legal full texts exist for public-domain works (Project Gutenberg) and open textbooks (above). Use those when they fit.
- Never quote more than one short line from any book, and never reconstruct its content chapter by chapter.

## Citing

- In teaching turns: short form after the fact, e.g. "(SEP, 'Utilitarianism')" or "(Python docs, 3.12, 'for statements')", with a link where possible.
- In the end-of-session artifact: a **Sources** list with title, publisher or site, and link. Use the learner's required citation style if they have one.
